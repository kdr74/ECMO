Distal Limb Perfusion & Distal Perfusion Cannula

Prevention and Management of Limb Ischaemia on VA-ECMO

Overview

Limb ischaemia is a serious complication of peripheral veno-arterial (VA) ECMO, occurring in 10-30% of patients. The large-bore arterial return cannula can significantly reduce or completely occlude antegrade blood flow to the distal limb, leading to acute ischaemia.

Key principle: Prevention is better than rescue. Proactive insertion of a distal perfusion cannula (DPC) at the time of ECMO cannulation reduces limb ischaemia rates significantly.

Critical Complication: Untreated limb ischaemia can lead to compartment syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, renal failure, amputation, and death. Early recognition and intervention are essential.

Pathophysiology

Mechanisms of Limb Ischaemia

  1. Arterial cannula occlusion: Large-bore (15-21 Fr) arterial return cannula occupies significant space in femoral artery, reducing antegrade flow
  2. Thrombosis: Arterial cannula promotes local thrombus formation
  3. Arterial spasm: Vessel spasm from mechanical irritation
  4. Compartment syndrome: Venous congestion and oedema compress arterial inflow (can occur even with adequate arterial perfusion)
  5. Pre-existing peripheral arterial disease (PAD): Patients with PAD have limited collateral circulation

Risk Factors

  • Small vessel size: Femoral artery diameter <6 mm
  • Female sex: Generally smaller vessels
  • Peripheral arterial disease: Poor collateral circulation
  • Large cannula size: 17-21 Fr cannulae
  • Prolonged ECMO duration: >7 days
  • Diabetes mellitus: Microvascular disease
  • Hypotension/low flow states: Reduced collateral perfusion

Distal Perfusion Cannula (DPC)

Indications

Universal recommendation: All patients on peripheral VA-ECMO should have a DPC inserted at the time of arterial cannulation, or as soon as possible thereafter.

Absolute Indications (Insert Immediately):

  • Arterial cannula ≥17 Fr
  • Small femoral artery (<6-7 mm diameter)
  • Known peripheral arterial disease
  • Female patients (higher risk of small vessels)
  • Any signs of limb ischaemia post-cannulation

Relative Indications (Consider Prophylactically):

  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Expected prolonged ECMO duration (>3-5 days)
  • Hypotension or low cardiac output state
  • Anticoagulation contraindications (higher thrombosis risk)

DPC Insertion Technique

Timing

Ideal: At time of ECMO cannulation (antegrade wire placed before arterial cannula insertion)

Acceptable: Within 2-4 hours of ECMO initiation

Emergency: Immediate if signs of limb ischaemia develop

Equipment

  • 6-9 Fr armoured (reinforced) sheath - preferred
  • 6-8 Fr standard angiography sheath - acceptable as temporary measure
  • 0.035" guidewire (standard or hydrophilic)
  • Micropuncture kit (helpful for small vessels)
  • Ultrasound guidance

Procedure Steps

Method 1: At Time of Initial ECMO Cannulation (Preferred)

  1. After inserting arterial sheath, BEFORE inserting arterial cannula
  2. Insert guidewire antegrade into ipsilateral superficial femoral artery (SFA)
  3. Secure wire with towel clip OR replace with 4-6 Fr sheath temporarily
  4. Proceed with ECMO cannula insertion
  5. Once ECMO established, insert DPC over the preserved wire

Method 2: After ECMO Cannulation

  1. Prepare: Male-to-male luer adaptor, 50 ml syringe, armoured sheath, clamps
  2. Using ultrasound, identify SFA distal to arterial cannula insertion site
  3. Micropuncture access to SFA (antegrade direction)
  4. Advance guidewire 10-15 cm into SFA
  5. Dilate and insert armoured sheath over wire
  6. Connect to ECMO circuit via side port (see connection procedure below)
Tip: If SFA access difficult, interventional cardiology team can help by crossing from contralateral SFA via aortic bifurcation, providing wire target for ipsilateral puncture.

DPC Connection to ECMO Circuit

The DPC must be connected to the arterial return limb of the ECMO circuit post-oxygenator to provide oxygenated blood to the distal limb.

Connection Procedure (From Main PDF Document):

  1. Prepare equipment:
    • Short male-to-male luer adaptor
    • 50 ml luer syringe
    • Two tubing clamps
  2. De-air DPC: Bleed back at 3-way tap until air-free
  3. Connect male-to-male adaptor: Attach to inline luer port of DPC 3-way tap
  4. Attach 50 ml syringe: To other luer port of DPC 3-way tap
  5. WARN TEAM: Patient will be off ECMO during next steps
  6. Clamp ECMO arterial return: Place clamps either side of the side luer port connector on arterial cannula (avoid clamping wire reinforcement)
  7. Keep ECMO pump circulating: Select "Global Override" on CardioHelp to prevent alarms
  8. Remove side port cap: From ECMO arterial return cannula
  9. Attach male-to-male adaptor: Connect DPC system to ECMO arterial cannula side port
  10. Remove proximal clamp: BEFORE the side luer port
  11. Aspirate air: Use 50 ml syringe to remove air from male-to-male connector until blood-filled
  12. Turn DPC 3-way tap OFF: To 50 ml syringe port
  13. Remove distal clamp: AFTER the side luer port - VA-ECMO support now restored
  14. Deactivate Global Override on CardioHelp
Result: Oxygenated blood now flows from ECMO arterial return through side port into DPC, perfusing distal limb.

Monitoring Distal Limb Perfusion

All sedated patients on peripheral VA-ECMO require continuous limb perfusion monitoring.

1. Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) - Primary Method

  • Bilateral lower limb NIRS: Applied to both calves
  • Target: Absolute rSO₂ >50%
  • Significant drop: <50% absolute OR >15% difference between limbs
  • Action threshold: Sustained drop >10-15% from baseline

2. Clinical Assessment

Frequency: Every 1-2 hours, more frequently if concerns

5 Ps of Limb Ischaemia:

Sign Assessment Severity Indicator
Pain Ask patient (if awake), observe for grimacing Early sign
Pallor Compare colour to contralateral limb Early sign
Pulselessness Doppler posterior tibial/dorsalis pedis arteries Significant
Paraesthesia Sensory testing (if awake) Advanced
Paralysis Motor function testing Late/severe
Compartment Syndrome Signs: Tense, swollen calf; pain on passive dorsiflexion; pressures >30 mmHg. This is a surgical emergency requiring immediate fasciotomy.

3. DPC Aspiration and Flushing

Purpose: Maintain DPC patency and assess distal perfusion

Technique:

  1. Step 1: Aspirate 3-5 ml blood from DPC via sheath 3-way tap - discard
  2. Step 2: Aspirate 2 ml blood from arterial cannula via 3-way tap with 2 ml syringe
  3. Step 3: Turn 3-way tap and flush this blood down the DPC

Frequency:

  • Minimum: Twice per 12-hour shift (every 6 hours)
  • Increased frequency: Hourly if clots aspirated or signs of compromised perfusion

Troubleshooting:

  • Clots aspirated: Increase flush frequency, check anticoagulation, consider thrombolysis
  • Unable to aspirate: DPC may be blocked - urgent intervention required
  • High resistance to flush: May indicate distal thrombosis or vessel spasm

Management of Limb Ischaemia

Immediate Actions (If Ischaemia Detected)

  1. Notify ECMO consultant immediately
  2. Optimize systemic perfusion:
    • Ensure adequate ECMO flow (≥3 L/min)
    • Maintain MAP >65 mmHg
    • Correct hypotension with fluids/vasopressors
  3. Check DPC (if present):
    • Confirm connection patent
    • Aspirate and flush DPC
    • Check for kinks or disconnection
  4. Optimize anticoagulation: Ensure therapeutic heparin (Anti-Xa 0.3-0.5)
  5. Consider thrombolysis: Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) via DPC if thrombosis suspected

Interventions by Severity

Mild Ischaemia (NIRS 40-50%, diminished pulses, no pain)

  • Optimize systemic perfusion
  • Insert DPC if not already present (urgent)
  • Increase DPC flush frequency
  • Close monitoring every 1-2 hours

Moderate Ischaemia (NIRS <40%, absent pulses, pain/pallor)

  • Insert DPC if not present (emergency procedure)
  • Consider catheter-directed thrombolysis via DPC
  • Vascular surgery consultation
  • Prepare for possible fasciotomy

Severe Ischaemia (NIRS <30%, compartment syndrome, paralysis)

  • Immediate vascular surgery intervention
  • Emergency fasciotomy if compartment syndrome
  • Consider ECMO cannula repositioning or alternative cannulation site
  • May require surgical cutdown and graft insertion

Alternative Strategies

If DPC Insertion Fails:

  • Retrograde posterior tibial artery cannulation: 24 cm length cannula needed
  • Vascular surgical cutdown: Direct exposure and cannulation of SFA
  • Change cannulation site: Consider contralateral femoral, axillary, or carotid artery

If DPC Inadequate Despite Correct Placement:

  • Larger DPC: Upsize from 6-7 Fr to 8-9 Fr
  • Catheter-directed thrombolysis: tPA infusion via DPC
  • Surgical bypass graft: Femoral artery side graft for arterial inflow

Compartment Syndrome

Definition

Compartment syndrome occurs when increased pressure within a muscle compartment compromises blood flow, leading to ischaemia and necrosis. Can occur even with adequate arterial perfusion due to venous congestion.

Risk Factors on VA-ECMO

  • Large venous drainage cannula (compression of femoral vein)
  • Reperfusion oedema after ischaemia
  • Coagulopathy with intramuscular bleeding
  • Prolonged limb ischaemia before DPC insertion

Clinical Signs

  • Pain: Severe, out of proportion to physical findings
  • Pain on passive stretch: Dorsiflexion of foot causes severe calf pain
  • Tense, swollen compartment: Palpably firm calf
  • Sensory loss: Reduced sensation in distribution of affected nerves
  • Motor weakness: Weakness or paralysis of foot/toes

Diagnosis

  • Clinical diagnosis: High index of suspicion essential
  • Compartment pressure measurement: >30 mmHg absolute OR <30 mmHg below diastolic BP
  • Creatine kinase (CK): Markedly elevated (>5000-10,000 U/L)

Management

SURGICAL EMERGENCY: Immediate fasciotomy required. Delays of >6 hours significantly increase risk of permanent disability and amputation.
  • Emergency vascular/orthopaedic surgery consultation
  • Four-compartment fasciotomy: Release all compartments of lower leg
  • Leave wounds open: Plan delayed closure or skin grafting
  • Optimize perfusion: Ensure adequate systemic and local blood flow
  • Monitor for rhabdomyolysis: IV fluids, monitor renal function and urine output

Decannulation Considerations

At the time of ECMO decannulation, formal vascular surgical closure of the arterial cannulation site is required.

Procedure

  • Location: Cardiac theatres (or vascular theatre if available)
  • Team: Vascular surgeon or cardiac surgeon trained in vascular repair
  • Steps:
    1. Surgical exposure of femoral artery
    2. Removal of arterial cannula
    3. Embolectomy (proximal and distal) to clear any thrombus
    4. Primary repair or patch angioplasty of arteriotomy
    5. Assessment of distal perfusion

DPC Removal

  • Remove DPC at same procedure as arterial decannulation
  • Manual pressure or purse-string suture for haemostasis
  • Confirm distal pulses present after removal

Key Points Summary

  • Universal DPC recommendation: Insert prophylactically in all peripheral VA-ECMO patients
  • Timing critical: Ideally at time of ECMO cannulation, within 2-4 hours at latest
  • Continuous monitoring essential: Bilateral lower limb NIRS plus clinical assessment every 1-2 hours
  • DPC maintenance: Aspirate and flush minimum twice per shift (6-hourly)
  • Early intervention saves limbs: Act immediately on NIRS <50% or clinical signs
  • Compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency: Fasciotomy within 6 hours essential
  • Vascular surgery involvement: Early consultation for moderate-severe ischaemia
  • Formal surgical closure required: Arterial repair by vascular/cardiac surgeon at decannulation
