LV Distention & Loss of Arterial Pulsatility

Recognition, Prevention and Management

Overview

Left ventricular (LV) distension is one of the most common and serious complications of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO). It occurs due to increased afterload from retrograde aortic flow, reduced myocardial contractility, and impaired ventricular ejection.

LV distension can lead to:

  • Pulmonary oedema and haemorrhage
  • Worsening myocardial ischaemia
  • Increased risk of intracardiac thrombus formation
  • Ventricular arrhythmias
  • Poor myocardial recovery
High-Risk Groups: Post-cardiotomy patients, patients with severe myocardial dysfunction, aortic regurgitation, and those with prolonged ECMO runs are at highest risk of LV distension.

Pathophysiology

Mechanisms of LV Distension on VA-ECMO

  1. Increased afterload: Retrograde aortic blood flow from the ECMO circuit increases LV afterload, making ejection more difficult
  2. Reduced contractility: The underlying cardiac pathology often involves severely reduced myocardial function
  3. Aortic valve closure: High afterload may prevent adequate aortic valve opening, leading to blood stasis in the LV
  4. Pulmonary venous return: Continued pulmonary venous return fills the LV despite poor ejection
  5. Mitral regurgitation: LV dilation can cause functional mitral regurgitation, worsening LV volume overload

Consequences

  • Pulmonary congestion: Elevated LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) causes pulmonary oedema and impaired gas exchange
  • Myocardial ischaemia: Increased wall stress and reduced coronary perfusion pressure worsen cardiac function
  • Thrombus formation: Blood stasis in the LV increases risk of thrombus, particularly in the apex and around the mitral valve
  • Arrhythmias: Ventricular stretch triggers arrhythmias, commonly ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation
  • Poor recovery: Chronic distension prevents myocardial recovery and limits chances of successful weaning

Detection and Monitoring

Proactive Approach Required: Certain aetiologies of cardiogenic shock are higher risk (myocarditis, E-CPR, AMI with extensive myocardial damage, post-cardiotomy with prolonged CPB times). In high-risk groups, a prophylactic approach with LV venting device insertion upfront may be preferred.

1. Echocardiography (Primary Tool)

Timing: Immediate post-ECMO initiation, then daily minimum (more frequently if concerns)

Key Parameters:

Parameter Threshold Significance
LVIDd (LV internal diameter end-diastole) >6.0 cm Absolute dilation
LVEDV/BSA (LV end-diastolic volume indexed) >100 ml/m² Significant volume overload
LVEF (LV ejection fraction) <10% Very severe dysfunction
LVOT VTI (LV outflow tract velocity time integral) <5 cm Minimal stroke volume
Aortic valve opening <1 per 3-4 beats Inadequate ejection
Mitral regurgitation >Mild Functional MR from dilation
Aortic regurgitation >Mild Worsens LV loading
Spontaneous echo contrast (SEC) Moderate-severe Blood stasis, thrombus risk

Important: Serial scanning is vital to detect dynamic changes from baseline. Even if absolute thresholds aren't met, progressive dilation with clinical suspicion warrants intervention.

2. Haemodynamic Monitoring

  • Arterial line waveform: Loss of pulsatility or pulse pressure <10 mmHg
  • Pulmonary artery catheter:
    • PADP or PCWP >18 mmHg (elevated LV filling pressures)
    • Low cardiac output despite high ECMO flows
  • Mixed venous saturation: SvO₂ <55% despite adequate ECMO support

3. Clinical Signs

  • Pink, frothy secretions from endotracheal tube
  • Worsening oxygenation and pulmonary infiltrates on chest X-ray
  • New ventricular arrhythmias (VT/VF)
  • Worsening shock indices despite increasing ECMO flows
  • Rising lactate or worsening metabolic acidosis

Management Strategies

EMERGENCY: Once LV distention is recognised, urgent unloading strategies should be undertaken. Target: achieve full unloading within 2 hours.

Medical Management (First-Line)

1. Reduce Afterload

  • Lower MAP targets: Reduce to 50-55 mmHg (from usual 55-60 mmHg)
  • Vasodilators: Consider sodium nitroprusside or GTN infusion
  • Wean vasopressors: Minimize noradrenaline dose
  • Reduce ECMO flow: Paradoxically, reducing flow may improve native cardiac output by reducing afterload (monitor closely for deteriorating shock indices)

2. Reduce Preload

  • Diuresis: Furosemide bolus or infusion if renal function adequate
  • Renal replacement therapy: Continuous veno-venous haemofiltration (CVVH) for fluid removal
  • Increase PEEP: Temporarily increase to 15-25 cmH₂O to reduce venous return (short-term only - may worsen RV function)

3. Optimize Contractility

  • Inotropes: Low-dose adrenaline (0.05-0.10 mcg/kg/min) or milrinone (0.25-0.50 mcg/kg/min)
  • Caution: If LV is non-responsive to inotropes, they may worsen distension by increasing RV output without improving LV ejection
  • Maintain pulsatility: Target arterial pulse pressure >10 mmHg

Mechanical LV Venting (Definitive Treatment)

Indications: Medical management fails to improve LV distension within 1-2 hours, or severe distension at presentation.

Options for Mechanical Unloading:

1. Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP)
  • Mechanism: Afterload reduction via diastolic augmentation
  • Role: May assist patients at risk of distension as prophylaxis
  • Limitation: NOT effective treatment for established progressive LV distension
  • Insertion: Percutaneous via femoral artery
2. Impella CP/5.0 (Percutaneous LVAD) - PREFERRED
  • Mechanism: Direct LV decompression by transaortic micro-axial pump
  • Advantages:
    • Provides both LV unloading and some circulatory support
    • Can be inserted percutaneously
    • Allows graded adjustment of support (P0-P9)
  • P-level titration: Start at P2-4, increase as needed for unloading
  • Monitoring: Assess LV dimensions and aortic valve opening with each P-level change
3. Percutaneous Pulmonary Artery Venting
  • Mechanism: Single-lumen cannula in PA drains into venous limb of ECMO circuit
  • Advantage: Reduces pulmonary venous return to LV
  • Limitation: Requires additional venous access and circuit modification
4. Surgical Options
  • Trans-apical LV vent: Direct LV apex cannulation draining to ECMO circuit
  • Atrial septostomy ± LA vent: Blade/balloon septostomy to decompress LA/LV
  • Indication: When percutaneous options not feasible or ineffective
  • Consideration: Requires return to theatre, increased bleeding risk

Loss of Arterial Pulsatility

Definition

Loss of arterial pulsatility refers to minimal or absent variation in arterial pressure waveform, with pulse pressure <10 mmHg. This reflects inadequate native cardiac ejection during VA-ECMO support.

Causes

  • Artefact: Damped arterial line, transducer malposition
  • Hypovolaemia: Excessive ECMO drainage, haemorrhage
  • Mechanical obstruction: Tamponade, tension pneumothorax, PE
  • Severe myocardial dysfunction: Primary cause - inability to generate forward flow
  • Increased afterload: High MAP targets, excessive vasoconstrictor use
  • Arrhythmias: VT, VF, severe bradycardia
  • Valvular dysfunction: Severe aortic or mitral regurgitation

Consequences

  • Thrombus formation: Blood stasis increases risk of massive intracardiac thrombosis (potentially lethal) or thromboembolism (stroke, limb, gut, kidney ischaemia)
  • Coronary hypoperfusion: Reduced diastolic coronary flow worsens myocardial ischaemia
  • LV distension: Loss of ejection leads to progressive LV dilation
  • Pulmonary oedema: Elevated LV pressures cause pulmonary congestion
Note: In E-CPR and severe acute cardiac failure, it is common to lose pulsatility immediately after commencing VA-ECMO. The absence of electrical activity or pulse pressure in the first few hours does not necessarily confer poor prognosis.

Assessment

  1. Exclude artefact: Check arterial line for damping, flush, palpate peripheral pulses
  2. Review ECMO settings: Check flow rate, assess for venous access insufficiency
  3. Check for mechanical causes:
    • Tension pneumothorax: Ventilation pressures, auscultation, CXR/ultrasound
    • Tamponade: Echocardiography, CVP measurement
    • Bleeding: Drains, Hb trend, coagulation screen
  4. Assess arrhythmias: ECG monitoring, treat non-perfusing rhythms
  5. Echocardiography: Comprehensive assessment as per LV distension section above

Management

  • Treat underlying cause: Volume resuscitation, drain tamponade, correct arrhythmias
  • Optimize afterload: Target MAP 50-55 mmHg, consider vasodilators
  • Inotropic support: Maintain pulsatility target (pulse pressure >10 mmHg)
  • Consider LV venting: If medical management ineffective after 1-2 hours
  • Increase anticoagulation: If spontaneous echo contrast or thrombus seen (balance against bleeding risk)

Prevention Strategies

  • Early LV venting in high-risk patients: Consider prophylactic Impella or IABP in E-CPR, severe LV failure, post-cardiotomy
  • Regular echocardiography: Daily minimum, more frequently if concerns
  • Maintain pulsatility: Start inotropes early to preserve some native cardiac output
  • Avoid excessive afterload: Keep MAP targets modest (55-60 mmHg), minimize vasopressors
  • Optimize ECMO flow: Lowest flow compatible with adequate perfusion (typically 2-3 L/min)
  • Aggressive diuresis/ultrafiltration: Prevent volume overload
  • Haemodynamic monitoring: Consider PA catheter for PCWP monitoring

Key Points Summary

  • LV distension is a common, serious complication of VA-ECMO requiring urgent intervention
  • High-risk patients should have prophylactic LV venting considered at ECMO initiation
  • Daily echocardiography is mandatory - serial assessment more important than absolute values
  • Medical management (afterload reduction, diuresis, inotropes) should be attempted first
  • Mechanical LV venting (Impella preferred) should be implemented within 2 hours if medical management fails
  • Loss of pulsatility increases thrombus risk and worsens myocardial recovery - maintain target pulse pressure >10 mmHg
  • Prevention is better than cure: early recognition and intervention improve outcomes
