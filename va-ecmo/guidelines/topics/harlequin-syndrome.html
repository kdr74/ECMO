Harlequin Syndrome (Differential Hypoxia)

Pathophysiology, Recognition and Management

Overview

Harlequin syndrome, also known as differential hypoxia or North-South syndrome, is a complication unique to peripheral veno-arterial (VA) ECMO. It occurs when deoxygenated blood ejected from the native heart perfuses the upper body (including the brain and coronary arteries), while oxygenated blood from the ECMO circuit perfuses the lower body.

This creates a situation where:

  • The upper body receives hypoxic blood from the recovering native heart
  • The lower body receives oxygenated blood from the ECMO return cannula
Clinical Significance: While counterintuitive, Harlequin syndrome often indicates recovering native cardiac function. However, it can lead to cerebral and myocardial hypoxia if inadequately managed.

Pathophysiology

Blood Flow Zones in VA-ECMO

Understanding Harlequin syndrome requires knowledge of the three blood flow zones during peripheral VA-ECMO:

  1. Upper body (head, right arm, coronary arteries):
    • Perfused by blood ejected from the left ventricle
    • Blood oxygenated by the native lungs
    • Quality depends on native cardiac output and pulmonary gas exchange
  2. Aortic arch (mixing zone):
    • Variable mixing of native cardiac output and ECMO flow
    • Mixing point moves with changes in cardiac function
  3. Lower body (lower chest, abdomen, legs):
    • Perfused by retrograde flow from ECMO femoral arterial cannula
    • Blood fully oxygenated by ECMO membrane

When Does Harlequin Syndrome Occur?

Harlequin syndrome develops when two conditions exist simultaneously:

  1. Recovering native cardiac function: LV generates significant forward flow (cardiac output)
  2. Impaired native pulmonary gas exchange: Blood passing through native lungs is inadequately oxygenated

This creates the paradox: The heart is recovering enough to eject blood, but the lungs cannot adequately oxygenate it.

Factors Influencing Mixing Zone Location

  • Native cardiac output: Higher output pushes mixing zone distally (towards legs)
  • ECMO flow rate: Higher ECMO flow pushes mixing zone proximally (towards head)
  • Systemic vascular resistance: Higher SVR favors ECMO flow distribution
  • Aortic valve function: Competent valve vs regurgitant valve changes flow dynamics

Clinical Presentation and Detection

Signs and Symptoms

  • Differential cyanosis: Upper body (especially right arm) appears more cyanotic than lower body (may be subtle with sedation)
  • Right arm SpO₂ significantly lower than lower limb SpO₂ (typically >5-10% difference)
  • Worsening neurological status if awake/sedated (agitation, confusion, altered consciousness)
  • Rising lactate despite adequate ECMO flows
  • Myocardial ischaemia: ECG changes, arrhythmias, troponin rise

Monitoring Strategy

1. Arterial Blood Gas Analysis

Critical requirement: Right radial or right brachial arterial line

  • Right upper limb gas: Reflects upper body oxygenation (brain, coronaries)
  • Lower limb gas (or femoral line): Reflects ECMO circuit oxygenation
  • Comparison identifies differential hypoxia
Parameter Right Arm Lower Body/Femoral Interpretation
PaO₂ <8-10 kPa 12-20 kPa Harlequin syndrome
SpO₂ <88-92% 95-100% Differential hypoxia

2. Pulse Oximetry

  • Right upper limb probe: Continuously monitor upper body oxygenation
  • Left upper limb or lower limb probe: May show higher saturations
  • Target: Right arm SpO₂ >88-90% minimum (preferably >92%)

3. Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS)

  • Bilateral cerebral NIRS: May show reduced cerebral oxygen saturation
  • Target: Cerebral rSO₂ >55-60%
  • Asymmetry: May indicate unilateral perfusion issues
Important: Relative hypoxia without end-organ dysfunction (normal lactate, no neurological signs, no arrhythmias) can be tolerated. Close monitoring of right arm SpO₂ and right-sided cerebral NIRS is essential.

Management Strategies

Step 1: Optimize Native Lung Function

Goal: Improve oxygenation of blood passing through native lungs

Ventilator Management:

  • Increase FiO₂: Target native lung FiO₂ 60-100%
  • Optimize PEEP: Recruit atelectatic lung (typically 10-15 cmH₂O)
  • Lung protective ventilation: Maintain Vt 6-8 ml/kg, driving pressure <14 cmH₂O
  • Consider recruitment manoeuvres if safe
  • Prone positioning: May improve V/Q matching in severe cases

Pulmonary Oedema Management:

  • Aggressive diuresis: Reduce pulmonary venous congestion
  • Renal replacement therapy: CVVH for fluid removal if needed
  • Optimize LV function: Prevent LV distension (see LV Distention guideline)

Adjunctive Therapies:

  • Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO): 10-20 ppm to improve V/Q matching
  • Inhaled prostacyclin: Alternative pulmonary vasodilator

Step 2: Modify ECMO Configuration

If LVEDP is LOW (Unlikely Pulmonary Dysfunction from LV Back Pressure):

Option A: Reduce Native Cardiac Output

  • Reduce Impella support: Lower P level if Impella in situ
  • Reduce inotropes: Decrease adrenaline, milrinone doses
  • Increase ECMO flow: This increases afterload, reducing native ejection
CAUTION: Risk of LV overdistention. Monitor closely with echocardiography. May worsen pulmonary congestion if LVEDP rises.

If LVEDP is HIGH (Likely Pulmonary Dysfunction from LV Back Pressure):

Option B: Increase LV Unloading

  • Increase Impella support: Higher P level to decompress LV
  • Increase inotropes: Improve LV contractility to reduce LVEDP
  • Reduce ECMO flow: Lower afterload to facilitate LV ejection
CAUTION: Risk of worsening Harlequin syndrome if this doesn't improve pulmonary gas exchange. May lead to hypotension and end-organ hypoperfusion.

Step 3: Change ECMO Configuration

If conservative measures fail and cerebral/myocardial hypoxia persists, consider ECMO configuration changes. These should be performed in theatre/cath lab with full team present.

Option 1: Convert to VV-ECMO (If Cardiac Function Adequate)

  • Indication: Native heart has recovered sufficiently to pass a VA-ECMO weaning study
  • Method: Insert new venous return cannula (IJ or femoral), decannulate arterial return
  • Advantage: Eliminates differential hypoxia, provides respiratory support only
  • Assessment required: Formal weaning study prior to conversion

Option 2: Convert to V-AV ECMO (If Cardiac Function Inadequate)

  • Indication: Native heart has NOT recovered enough to pass weaning study
  • Method: Insert additional venous return cannula (IJ typically), connect to ECMO circuit post-oxygenator with Y-connector
  • Result: Oxygenated blood delivered to both RV (via IJ) and descending aorta (via femoral)
  • Titration: Adjust flow ratio between RV and aortic return using inline gates

Option 3: Carotid Artery Cannulation

  • Indication: Refractory differential hypoxia despite all other measures
  • Method: Direct cannulation of right common carotid artery (surgical)
  • Advantage: Guarantees oxygenated blood to upper body
  • Disadvantage: Invasive, requires surgical expertise, stroke risk

Clinical Decision Algorithm

Step-by-Step Approach:

  1. Detect: Right arm SpO₂ <90%, PaO₂ difference >3-4 kPa, or cerebral NIRS <55%
  2. Assess severity: Check for end-organ dysfunction (lactate, neurological signs, arrhythmias)
  3. Optimize lungs: Increase FiO₂, optimize PEEP, diurese, consider iNO
  4. Assess LVEDP: Use echocardiography and/or PA catheter
    • If LVEDP low → Consider reducing native output (reduce inotropes/Impella, increase ECMO flow)
    • If LVEDP high → Increase LV unloading (increase Impella, increase inotropes, reduce ECMO flow)
  5. If refractory: Consider ECMO configuration change
    • VV-ECMO if cardiac function recovered
    • V-AV ECMO if cardiac function not recovered
    • Carotid cannulation as last resort

Acceptable vs Unacceptable Hypoxia

Tolerable Differential Hypoxia

Relative hypoxia can be accepted if:

  • Right arm SpO₂ 88-92% (PaO₂ >8 kPa)
  • Cerebral NIRS >55%
  • No rising lactate (<2 mmol/L)
  • No neurological deterioration
  • No myocardial ischaemia (ECG stable, no new arrhythmias)
  • Improving cardiac function on echocardiography

Unacceptable Hypoxia Requiring Urgent Intervention

  • Right arm SpO₂ <85% persistently
  • Cerebral NIRS <50% or declining trend
  • Rising lactate (>2 mmol/L or increasing)
  • Neurological deterioration (agitation, confusion, seizures)
  • Myocardial ischaemia (ST changes, VT/VF, rising troponin)
  • Worsening cardiac function despite ECMO support
Clinical Judgement Required: Balance the degree of hypoxia against the benefit of allowing native cardiac recovery. Close collaboration with ECMO consultants and cardiology team is essential.

Prevention Strategies

  • Maintain lung-protective ventilation: Even on VA-ECMO, continue lung protection
  • Aggressive pulmonary oedema management: Early diuresis, avoid positive fluid balance
  • Right arm arterial line: Always insert to monitor upper body oxygenation
  • Cerebral NIRS monitoring: Detect cerebral hypoxia early
  • Regular arterial blood gases: From right arm and lower body/femoral line
  • Early detection of cardiac recovery: Daily echocardiography to assess LV function improvement
  • Avoid excessive ECMO flow reduction: Gradual weaning with close monitoring

Key Points Summary

  • Harlequin syndrome occurs when recovering native cardiac function ejects hypoxic blood to upper body while ECMO perfuses lower body with oxygenated blood
  • It often indicates improving cardiac function - a positive sign
  • Right radial/brachial arterial line is mandatory for monitoring
  • Optimize native lung function first: FiO₂, PEEP, diuresis, consider iNO
  • LVEDP assessment guides management: reduce native output if low, increase LV unloading if high
  • Relative hypoxia (SpO₂ 88-92%) without end-organ dysfunction can be tolerated
  • Severe refractory hypoxia may require ECMO configuration change (VV or V-AV)
  • Prevention through lung-protective ventilation and pulmonary oedema management
